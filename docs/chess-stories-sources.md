# PART A — 20 story candidates (+6 mini-cards)

## A1. Caissa — the goddess somebody made up

- **Hook:** Who is the goddess of chess — and was she ever worshipped?
- **Facts:** (1) Marco Girolamo Vida's Latin poem _Scacchia, Ludus_ (658 lines, pub. 1527): Apollo and Mercury play chess before the gods; a chess-dryad "Schacchia" is present; an unauthorised 742-line draft leaked in 1525. (2) William Jones (b. 1746) wrote the English poem _Caïssa, or The Game at Chess_ in 1763 "at the age of 17". (3) Plot: nymph Caïssa rejects Mars, god of war; Mars asks "Euphron, God of Sport (Jones's invention)", who creates chess as a gift to win her. (4) Jones later founded the Asiatic Society in Calcutta (15 Jan 1784) and in 1786 suggested Sanskrit, Greek and Latin share a common root; in Paris he tried, with Benjamin Franklin, to resolve the American Revolution (fruitless). (5) Philidor's English edition (1777) printed Jones's poem. (6) That Jones's "Caïssa" equals Vida's "Scacchia" is "an unproven assumption". (7) The Soviet program **Kaissa** won the first World Computer Chess Championship (1974). (8) Kasparov uses "Caissa was with me"; in _How Life Imitates Chess_ p.188 "Caissa... had punished me for my conservative play".
- **Legend vs documented:** Caissa is a Renaissance/18th-century literary invention, not an ancient Greek myth. Poems and their dates are documented.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ca%C3%AFssa (Vida/Jones, 658 lines, 1527, "unproven assumption", Kaissa 1974); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Jones_(philologist) (poem at 17, Asiatic Society, Sanskrit 1786, Franklin).
- **Mind-changing idea:** In the poem the god of war is won not by force but by a _game_ — chess as the art of turning conflict into play. And: the "ancient goddess" is a teenager's poem.
- **Kid angle:** "A 17-year-old invented the chess goddess. Write your own legend of a piece." **Confidence:** high (facts), the myth-reading is interpretation.

## A2. Franklin's "The Morals of Chess"

- **Hook:** Can a chess game teach you to be careful, hopeful and kind?
- **Facts:** (1) First printed in _The Columbian Magazine_, Dec 1786 (vol.1 no.3, pp.158-160). (2) Franklin Papers vol. 29 date the text "before 28 June 1779" (written in Passy, France); the editors argue the ideas go back to 1732 [snippet]. No copy of the French imprint survives. (3) Franklin "played chess from at least 1733", was above-average but not top level (WP); US Chess Hall of Fame 1999. (4) The essay names three habits: **Foresight** ("If I move this piece, what will be the advantages of my new situation? What use can my adversary make of it to annoy me?"), **Circumspection** (survey the whole board), **Caution** (not moving hastily — taught by the touch-move rule; this rule is still FIDE Law 4.3). (5) Fourth habit: "not being discouraged by present bad appearances... hoping for a favorable change... persevering in the search of resources"; and success breeds "presumption... inattention" while misfortune brings "more care". (6) Etiquette: no false moves, no hurrying, no gloating, console the loser, spectators silent; and "moderate your desire of victory... be pleased with one over yourself". (7) Autobiography: in 1733 he and a friend learning Italian played chess where the winner set a task — "we thus beat one another into that language". (8) Appeared in Russia in the first Russian chess book (1791) (WP).
- **Legend vs documented:** The text is real. The "bath" anecdote (Madame Brillon watched Franklin play from her bathtub; he apologised by letter, 1784) is documented in Franklin's letter but she was his romantic friend — **[PARENT]**/skip.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Morals_of_Chess_(1841) (full text opened); https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/148/pg148.txt (Autobiography, Italian passage); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Morals_of_Chess (publication data); https://www.historynet.com/benjamin-franklin-diplomat-libertine-chess-master/ (1779, no French copy, bath letter).
- **Mind-changing idea:** Chess trains life habits (look ahead, look around, slow down, don't give up), and the best way to win is to stay kind.
- **Kid angle:** "Franklin's 3 super-powers: Foresight, Circumspection, Caution." Link to math: "plan the consequences." **Confidence:** high.

## A3. Timur's Great Chess (Uzbek heritage)

- **Hook:** What if every pawn had its own secret future?
- **Facts:** (1) "Great chess / Tamerlane chess" is a large-board shatranj variant of Central Asia/Persia. Board per _Nafa'is al-funun_: 110 squares (10x11) + 2 citadels = 112. (2) 11 kinds of pawns — each pawn is "the pawn of" a piece (camel, elephant, giraffe, king...) and promotes to that piece. (3) New pieces: giraffe, camel, "war engine" (dabbaba), scout. (4) Stalemating the enemy king is a win; once per game a king may swap places with another piece; entering the enemy citadel draws. (5) Timur (born 1320s near Kesh = Shahrisabz, died 17/18 Feb 1405; buried in Gur-e-Amir, Samarkand) "loved to play chess" and preferred large boards; he invited top players such as Ali ash-Shatranji of Tabriz — per WP. (6) Ibn Arabshah (15th c.): Timur was in a chess game when told of the birth of his youngest son (born 20 Aug 1377) and named him **Shah Rukh** (Persian for the chess move/castling — "check-rook"). (7) British Museum MS 7322 reproduces Arabshah's work with a setup. (8) Modern clubs revive it (Tamerlane Chess Club, Jamestown NY; ChessBase 22 Jan 2023).
- **Legend vs documented:** "Timur invented it" = tradition ("attributed"). Shah Rukh naming = one chronicler's anecdote (Arabshah was a critical biographer [snippet]). Documented: such big-board variants existed and were played in Timurid lands. Not verified: exact date of _Nafa'is al-funun_. Note: Timur's campaigns included massacres (WP) — keep the story about the game.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_chess (rules, Ali ash-Shatranji, 110/112 squares); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shah_Rukh_(Timurid_dynasty) (birth 1377, Arabshah chess anecdote); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timur (dates, Kesh, Gur-e-Amir, "Tamerlane chess on a 10x11 board").
- **Mind-changing idea:** Chess is not one frozen game — people have always redesigned the board and the rules; a pawn's "destiny" can differ.
- **Kid angle:** "Design your own pawn of a camel — where does it promote?" **Confidence:** medium (variants high; Timur-inventor low).

## A8. The Polgár experiment

- **Hook:** Can you _train_ genius? A father bet three daughters' childhoods on it.
- **Facts:** (1) László Polgár (b. 1946), educational psychologist; thesis "geniuses are made, not born". He studied biographies of 400 great intellectuals (People, 1987). Experiment began 1970 per WP: start before age 3, specialise at 6. (2) Daughters Susan (Zsuzsa), Sofia, Judit were home-schooled (chess plus Esperanto, German, Russian, English, high-level maths); he fought Hungarian authorities for permission. (3) Judit b. 23 July 1976; at 5 beat a family friend blindfold — friend joked "You are good at chess, but I'm a good cook"; Judit: "Do you cook without looking at the stove?" (4) Jan 1989: world No. 55 at 12, the youngest then in the top 100; **Dec 1991 GM at 15 y 4 m — beating Fischer's record by one month**; only woman in the world top 10 (Jan 1996), peak rating 2735 (2005); beat Kasparov in a rapid game 2002 (Moscow); retired Aug 2014. (5) Susan became Women's World Champion; Sofia (IM) left chess. (6) Judit in 2008: "People would say, 'The parents are destroying them... they have no childhood'. I became defensive, and not very sociable." (7) Kasparov, early on, doubted women ("No woman can sustain a prolonged battle") but later: "The Polgárs showed that there are no inherent limitations to their aptitude" (the 2002 loss).
- **Honest caveat:** n = 3, no control group — it shows what is _possible_, not that anyone can be trained to genius (see C2).
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judit_Polg%C3%A1r; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A1szl%C3%B3_Polg%C3%A1r; https://www.chess.com/article/view/school-chess-fails-pr-test (Judit's Hungarian school chess course).
- **Mind-changing idea:** Early, loving, intense practice can produce extraordinary results; and prejudices ("girls can't") fall to evidence, even for Kasparov.
- **Kid angle:** the stove line (humor, kid speaks). **Confidence:** high.

## A9. Vera Menchik and the "Vera Menchik Club"

- **Hook:** A joke made to ridicule her became her badge of honour.
- **Facts:** (1) b. Moscow 16 Feb 1906, Czech father/English mother; taught chess by her father at 9; moved to Hastings, England, 1921 with only Russian — chess needed little English. (2) First Women's World Champion (1927, age 21; 10.5/11) and held it until her death in 1944 — the longest reign (nearly 17 years); won at least 59 games in a row at women's world tournaments. (3) Played in men's master events; 1929 Ramsgate shared 2nd with Rubinstein. Beat Euwe twice (Hastings 1930/31 and 1931/32). (4) **Vera Menchik Club**: at Carlsbad 1929 Albert Becker, after she lost a game, joked that anyone who lost to her would be a "member"; he became the first member; Euwe and George Thomas were called "presidents"; six Grandmasters (e.g. Euwe, Reshevsky) are on the list. Hans Kmoch had said he would "go on stage as a ballerina" if she scored over 3 points (she scored exactly 3; he later regretted it). (5) Alekhine (1928): "She is without a doubt a phenomenon". (6) The Women's Olympiad winning-team trophy is the **Vera Menchik Cup** (since 1957); first woman in the World Chess Hall of Fame (2011). (7) Killed 26 June 1944 in a V-1 raid on London with her mother and sister **[PARENT]**.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vera_Menchik (all items).
- **Mind-changing idea:** Being the "first" among people who doubt you; the best answer to mockery is results — they renamed the mockery after you.
- **Kid angle:** "The club nobody wanted to join." **Confidence:** high.

## A10. Capablanca — the book that shows his own lost games

- **Hook:** Why did a world champion publish games he LOST?
- **Facts:** (1) b. Havana 19 Nov 1888; learned at 4 by watching his father, pointed out an illegal move, then beat him (per his own account); beat Cuban champion Corzo in 1901 (2 days before turning 13) but lost both games to Corzo at the 1902 national championship. (2) 1909 US tour: 602 simul games in 27 cities, 96.4%; 1922 Cleveland simul vs 103 players: 102 wins, 1 draw. (3) World Champion 1921-27 (beat Lasker; lost to Alekhine 1927: 6 wins, 3 losses, 25 draws for Alekhine). (4) "As an adult, lost only 34 serious games" (WP); unbeaten from 10 Feb 1916 to 21 Mar 1924 (63 games: 40 wins, 23 draws), lost then to Réti. (5) _Chess Fundamentals_ (1921; Gutenberg #33870): "From now on to the end of the book I shall give a collection of my games both lost and won..."; on a 1909 loss: "my total lack of knowledge of the different variations in this opening". (6) Alekhine on the young Capablanca: "flabbergasting quickness of chess comprehension... gave all the St. Petersburg masters the odds of 5-1 in quick games — and won!" (7) Botvinnik thought _Chess Fundamentals_ the best chess book ever. (8) Died 8 March 1942 in New York after a stroke.
- **Sources:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/33870/pg33870.txt (primary); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Ra%C3%BAl_Capablanca; https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Ra%C3%BAl_Capablanca (quote citations).
- **Mind-changing idea:** Honest losers learn faster; speed comes from knowing simple things deeply.
- **Kid angle:** "Make your own 'losses notebook'." **Confidence:** high (the "34 losses" figure is WP-secondary).

## A11. Mikhail Tal — "the Magician from Riga"

- **Hook:** Did Tal really not calculate everything?
- **Facts:** (1) b. Riga 9 Nov 1936; youngest USSR champion 1957 (age 20); GM title waived into him by FIDE 1957; **1960 World Champion at 23**, beating Botvinnik 12.5-8.5 (6 wins, 2 losses, 13 draws); lost the 1961 rematch (ill; kidney trouble). (2) Unbeaten streak of 95 games (23 Oct 1973-16 Oct 1974), 46 wins and 49 draws; 1988 World Blitz champion at 51. (3) Aged 11 in 1948 he visited the new champion Botvinnik hoping to play; Botvinnik's wife said he was asleep and resting from chess. (4) Style: sacrifices for initiative; post-game analysis found flaws in some calculations. (5) On fear (Life and Games of Mikhail Tal): "I began to succeed in decisive games, perhaps because I realised a very simple truth: not only was I worried, but so was my opponent." (6) Left a hospital to play a Moscow blitz on 28 May 1992 and beat Kasparov; died 28 June 1992 (WP) **[PARENT]**.
- **Quote status:** The "dark forest where 2+2=5" line is a **compressed popular version**; a longer passage ("dense forest of unexplored variations... a narrow mountain path... you sometimes have to try and demonstrate that two times two is five") is quoted by Kasparov in _My Great Predecessors_ vol.2 "without a source" (Exeter CC). Never present it as verified Tal.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Tal; https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Mikhail_Tal; https://exeterchessclub.org.uk/node/1887; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Botvinnik (child visit).
- **Mind-changing idea:** Your opponent is scared too — complexity is a weapon. **Kid angle:** "Be a magician: set one problem per move." **Confidence:** high (facts), quote W.

## A12. Emanuel Lasker — chess champion, mathematician, friend of Einstein

- **Hook:** Could the best chess mind also prove theorems?
- **Facts:** (1) b. 24 Dec 1868, Berlinchen; World Champion **27 years (1894-1921)**, the longest reign. (2) At 11 sent to Berlin to study maths; brother Berthold taught him chess. (3) 1895: two maths articles in _Nature_; on David Hilbert's advice a PhD (Erlangen, thesis 1901, doctorate 1902); 1905 theorem on primary decomposition, later generalised by Emmy Noether. Also played Go and bridge; invented the game Lasca. (4) Lost the 1921 match to Capablanca in Havana, resigning with "sic transit gloria mundi!". (5) 1935 Moscow, aged 66, third undefeated — "a biological miracle". (6) Friend of Einstein, who wrote the foreword to Hannak's 1952 biography. (7) Fled Nazi Germany 1933 (Jewish) **[PARENT]**; died New York 11 Jan 1941. (8) Jacques Hannak: Lasker looked for the move "most embarrassing for the specific person sitting on the other side of the board".
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emanuel_Lasker; https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Emanuel_Lasker (Einstein foreword, Hannak, Manual of Chess pages).
- **Mind-changing idea:** Chess and maths are siblings; chess is also a psychological conversation, not just "best move". **Kid angle:** "He proved things on paper and on the board." **Confidence:** high.

## A13. Kasparov, Deep Blue — and the "centaur"

- **Hook:** Did the computer win — or did a team of human + computer win later?
- **Facts:** (1) 1996 Philadelphia, 10-17 Feb: Kasparov won 4-2 but Deep Blue won game 1 — first computer win vs a reigning champion in a classical game. (2) 1997 New York, 3-11 May: Deep Blue 3.5-2.5; game 6 ended in 19 moves after a knight sacrifice. Kasparov alleged cheating after game 2 (a human-looking move); a bug in game 1 (move 44 fail-safe) is "allegedly" reported. (3) Prize: $700,000 winner/$400,000 loser (1997). (4) In 1998, in León, Kasparov created **Advanced Chess** (human + computer; vs Topalov, 3-3). (5) 2005 "freestyle" tournament on Playchess.com: winners were "a pair of amateur American chess players using three computers at the same time"; Kasparov: "Weak human + machine + better process was superior to a strong computer alone." (6) Foreword 2018: "a superior coordination process between them was more important than the strength of the human or the machine"; this "formula has an expiration date in a small, closed system like chess".
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_Blue_versus_Garry_Kasparov; https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2010/02/11/the-chess-master-and-the-computer/ (11 Feb 2010); Game Changer foreword excerpt https://dokumen.pub/game-changer-alphazeros-groundbreaking-chess-strategies-and-the-promise-of-ai-9789056918187.html.
- **Mind-changing idea:** The best player is neither "man" nor "machine", but the pair with the best _process_. **Kid angle:** "You + a tool beat the tool alone." **Confidence:** high.

## A14. AlphaZero — a student with no teachers

- **Hook:** What happens when a computer learns chess knowing only the rules?
- **Facts:** (1) DeepMind preprint 5 Dec 2017; trained by self-play, no opening books/endgame tables. (2) After 4 hours it was estimated stronger than Stockfish 8; after 9 hours won a 100-game match **28 wins, 72 draws, 0 losses**. (3) It searched about 80,000 positions/s vs 70 million for Stockfish. (4) Science paper 7 Dec 2018: 1000-game match 155 wins, 6 losses, 839 draws vs a stronger Stockfish setup. (5) Critics (Nakamura, Stockfish's Romstad) objected to hardware/time settings. (6) Kasparov (foreword, 2018): "I was quite happy to see AlphaZero's dynamic, sacrificial style... prefers piece activity and attacking chances"; but "It doesn't think in terms of sacrifices or anything else. It is simply playing what works best." Also: "Instead of a postcard from a far-off land, it's a telescope". (7) Demis Hassabis started chess at 4; says chess taught him to solve problems, plan, handle pressure and "imagine and visualise possible futures". (8) Sadler & Regan's _Game Changer_ (2019) analysed 2,000+ unpublished AlphaZero games.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AlphaZero; excerpt https://dokumen.pub/game-changer-alphazeros-groundbreaking-chess-strategies-and-the-promise-of-ai-9789056918187.html (foreword/preface);
- **Mind-changing idea:** Many "human rules" (pawns are precious) are habits, not laws; a self-taught mind found activity can beat material. **Kid angle:** "Learn by playing yourself." **Confidence:** high (facts); "style" claims = Kasparov/Sadler opinions.

## A15. Kasparov versus the World (1999)

- **Hook:** Can 50,000 strangers think like one player?
- **Facts:** Played online on MSN Gaming Zone, launched 21 June 1999; the World voted each move (plurality); >50,000 people from >75 countries; 62 moves over four months; Kasparov won. Teen advisers: Bacrot (16), Felecan (19), **Irina Krush (15, US Women's champion)**, Paehtz (14). Krush's recommendations were chosen every move from 10 to 50 and she built a shared analysis tree. Kasparov: "It is the greatest game in the history of chess" and he "never expended as much effort on any other game". An earlier 1996 voting game vs Karpov was an easy win for Karpov.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kasparov_versus_the_World. **Mind-changing idea:** collaboration + a good leader beats a lone genius almost. **Confidence:** high.

## A16. Chess in space — Soyuz 9 (1970)

- **Hook:** How do you keep pieces from floating away?
- **Facts:** Soyuz 9 (Nikolayev, Sevastyanov): launched 1 June 1970, landed 19 June (~18 days, endurance record). On **9 June 1970** they played Earth (Kamanin, Gorbatko) — the first documented chess game by humans in space; 35 moves; a draw (Queen's Gambit Accepted). The space set was designed by Mikhail Klevtsov with grooves and rails (magnets ruled out for equipment). Sevastyanov joked pieces could fly "into a sleeping astronaut's mouth". Radio exchange (FIDE museum): "Earth, you're taking too long. Your time is up! — We still have a full minute!".
- **Sources:** https://museum.fide.com/exhibits/the-first-ever-chess-set-to-have-traveled-to-outer-space; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soyuz_9 (date, draw, 35...Kg8). **Mind-changing idea:** the rules of chess survive weightlessness; engineering for a game. **Confidence:** high.

## A17. Lewis chessmen — pieces with faces

- **Hook:** Which chess piece bites its shield?
- **Facts:** Found 1831 on the Isle of Lewis (Outer Hebrides); 94 objects (78 chess pieces, 14 tablemen, 1 buckle); walrus ivory (some whale teeth); made c.1150-1200, "commonly believed" Trondheim; now 82 pieces British Museum, 11 National Museum of Scotland; a missing "warder" resurfaced 2019 and sold for £735,000. Rooks = warders; four are berserkers biting shields; kings crowned and swordsmen; queens seated with a hand on the cheek (read as "contemplation... possibly wisdom"); bishops with croziers; pawns plain geometric shapes. BM paid 80 guineas. Red stain suggests red/white sides.
- **Legend vs documented:** Finder name Malcolm MacLeod appears first in 1863; the "found by a cow" tale and exact spot (Uig vs Mealista) are uncertain. Facial "comic" look = modern reading.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_chessmen; museum pages (BM blocked). **Mind-changing idea:** Old chess was a picture of society; the bishop is a churchman. **Confidence:** high.

## A18. Harun al-Rashid, Charlemagne — a chess legend that doesn't hold

- **Hook:** Did a caliph send a chess set to a king?
- **Facts:** The "Charlemagne chessmen" (Bibliothèque nationale de France): ivory, made c.1050-1100 in Salerno/south Italy, 200+ years after Charlemagne (d.814); 30 pieces in 1598, 16 survive. Legend: gift of Caliph Harun al-Rashid (embassy 802). Notker the Stammerer (880s) lists an elephant but no chess set; Einhard and the Royal Frankish Annals mention the elephant **Abul-Abbas** (real). Only the "Elephant of Charlemagne" piece is 9th-century Indian and may have come with it. A rival legend (Empress Irene) also fails. Another Charlemagne legend: playing chess when Roland's horn sounded. **Note conflict:** the WP Harun article lists "ivory chessmen" among the 802 gifts, which the Charlemagne article says the sources do not support.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlemagne_chessmen; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harun_al-Rashid. **Mind-changing idea:** check the source — a good story is not evidence. **Confidence:** medium (legend status high; Harun-and-chess otherwise unverified; as-Suli etc. already in app).

## A19. Blindfold chess: Philidor 1783, Najdorf 1943/1947

- **Hook:** Can you play chess without seeing the board — 45 times at once?
- **Facts:** (1) Philidor (composer and chess master, 1726-95), 9 May 1783, St James's Street club, London: three simultaneous blindfold games; affidavits were signed because people doubted future generations would believe. (2) Koltanowski set 34 boards in Edinburgh, 1937. (3) Miguel Najdorf: 1943 Rosario, 40 boards (36 wins, 1 draw, 3 losses); 1947 São Paulo, 45 boards (39 wins, 4 draws, 2 losses); Koltanowski doubted (scoresheets, several opponents per board), modern historians accept; record stood until Marc Lang's 46 boards in 2011. (4) Najdorf stayed in Argentina when WWII broke out during the 1939 Olympiad; he hoped the publicity would reach his family — they were murdered in the Holocaust **[PARENT]**.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blindfold_chess; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miguel_Najdorf; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois-Andr%C3%A9_Danican_Philidor. **Mind-changing idea:** chess lives in the mind's eye; extraordinary claims need witnesses. **Kid angle:** play one blindfold mini-game with a parent. **Confidence:** medium-high (Najdorf motive = WP only).

## A20. Zweig's _Chess Story_ (fiction!)

- **Facts:** Novella _Schachnovelle_ by Stefan Zweig, written 1941, published 1942 (Buenos Aires, Verlag Pigmalión); ship from New York to Buenos Aires; Dr B., held in isolation by the Gestapo, studies a stolen book of master games and learns to play himself; later beats champion Czentovic, then is worn down when Czentovic plays slowly to irritate him. The fragment of game cited is Alekhine–Bogoljubow, Pistyan 1922 (real). Zweig died 1942 **[PARENT]**.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Royal_Game. **Mind-changing idea:** chess can be a shelter but also a trap; slow play is a weapon (tie to A2 caution/B). **Suitability:** low for 6-12; use only as "a book for later". **Confidence:** high (facts), label **FICTION**.

# PART C — 12 "new way of seeing chess" ideas with evidence

**C1. "Chess is about winning" -> "chess is about learning from losses."** Capablanca's book shows his lost games (Gutenberg); Franklin: "be pleased with one over yourself"; Abdusattorov last place -> title (A5). Confidence high. https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/33870/pg33870.txt

**C2. "Only geniuses" vs practice.** Hambrick et al. (2014 _Intelligence_): deliberate practice explained ~34% of variance in chess skill (so ~66% other factors) [snippet]; Gobet & Campitelli: hours to master ranged 3,016-23,608 (avg ~11,000), and some with >25,000 never reached master [snippet via BPS digest https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/exploding-10000-hours-myth-its-no-guarantee-greatness]. Polgár sisters (n=3). Honest message: practice matters a lot, is not the whole story; people differ in pace. Botvinnik judged young Karpov harshly (X1). Confidence medium.

**C3. "Chess makes you smarter?" — be honest.** Sala & Gobet (2016, _Educational Research Review_): 24 studies, 40 effect sizes, g = 0.338 overall (maths 0.382, reading 0.248), bigger with more hours, 25-30 h as probable minimum; but nearly all studies lacked active control groups, so placebo/motivation effects can't be ruled out (Frontiers 2017). UK EEF trial (Jerrim et al., 22 July 2016): ~100 schools, Year 5 (age 9-10), 25-30 hours of lessons, tested a year later on national tests: **no difference** in maths, reading, science, also none for disadvantaged pupils (N ~1,965 vs 1,900 analysed); critics: 1-year delay, tiny dose, uneven teaching. Sala & Gobet 2017 (_Learning & Behavior_; N=233 + N=52, with checkers/Go as active controls): no significant maths-problem-solving gain; authors: effects "if any... modest". What _is_ supported: pupils and teachers liked it; teachers were "very positive about the intervention and its impact on pupils' skills and behavior", many kids kept playing a year later, ~1 in 10 disliked it (chess.com summary of EEF). Attention/planning gains: plausible, not demonstrated by the sources I opened (Gelfand/Hassabis are opinion). One 2019 IZA field-experiment abstract in a developing country reported maths gains (details not verified). Message for the app: "Chess is great for chess, fun, patience and company — not a proven shortcut to maths grades." Confidence high. https://www.chess.com/article/view/school-chess-fails-pr-test ; https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00238/full ; https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/102237 ; https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13420-017-0280-3 [snippet]

**C4. "A blunder is information."** Kramnik, world champion, overlooked mate in one (2006); Lasker lost the 1921 match after a blunder yet stayed a respected thinker and played at 66; Lasker lists "incapacity for admitting mistakes" among dead habits. Message: a blunder shows what you didn't see. Confidence high. https://en.chessbase.com/post/how-could-kramnik-overlook-the-mate-

**C5. "The computer didn't kill chess."** 1,961 players in Samarkand 2026 (A4); Carlsen's peak rating 2882 (2014) exceeded Kasparov's 1999 peak 2851 (cross-era rating comparisons are debated — medium); Kasparov: AlphaZero is a "telescope"; Freestyle/centaur (A13). Confidence medium. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garry_Kasparov

**C6. "Sacrifice = trade small now for bigger later."** Immortal Game (X2); AlphaZero sacrifices pawns for activity — but Kasparov: machines "don't think in terms of sacrifices"; the word is our label (A14). Confidence high.

**C7. "A pawn is a hero in disguise."** FIDE Laws 3.7.3.3: a pawn reaching the last rank must be exchanged for a queen, rook, bishop or knight; Capablanca: "The gain of a Pawn is the smallest material advantage... often is sufficient to win"; in Tamerlane chess each pawn promotes differently (A3). Confidence high. https://handbook.fide.com/chapter/E012023

**C8. "Chess is a conversation."** Franklin's foresight question asks what the _opponent_ will do with each move; Lasker chose the move most awkward for the specific opponent; Polgár preferred to learn an opponent's style; Kasparov vs the World was a conversation among thousands. Confidence medium-high.

**C9. "Chess is huge, not small" (maths).** About 10^120 games, 197,281 possible games after only 4 plies; 92 solutions to eight queens (X3). Confidence high.

**C10. "Chess is war?" — both/and.** Franklin: the touch-move rule makes chess "the image of human life, and particularly of war"; Kasparov (1995): "chess is a violent sport... mental torture" (Independent on Sunday, as quoted); Lewis berserkers vs a contemplative queen (A17). Yet the same sources demand courtesy: Franklin's etiquette, Gelfand "respecting their opponent", Gligorić trophy (X4). Message: the pieces are an army; the players are partners. Confidence medium.

**C11. "Fast vs slow."** Franklin: "Caution, not to make our moves too hastily"; touch-move is law (FIDE 4.3); Capablanca was famously fast because of knowledge (Alekhine, A10); in Zweig's fiction the champion wins by slowing the game (A20); Soyuz-9 radio banter about the clock (A16). Confidence medium-high.

**C12. "Kindness is part of the rules of the spirit."** Franklin's etiquette (1786), Kieseritzky praising the loser's opponent (X2), Menchik "club" (A9), 2026 fair-play trophies (X4). Confidence high.
